\section{Introduction}
Metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are widely used as electronic switches because the voltage applied to the gate controls the conductivity of the channel. When a positive gate voltage greater than threshold voltage is applied to an n-channel MOSFET, it establishes an electric field across the oxide, attracting electrons toward the oxide--semiconductor interface and forming a conductive channel. The oxide electrically separates the gate from the semiconductor and ideally prevents direct charge flow, while still allowing the gate to control the channel electrostatically.

Reducing the oxide thickness increases the oxide capacitance, allowing the gate voltage to control the channel charge more effectively. However, at the nanometer scale, a thinner oxide also means a narrower potential barrier. Thus, the tunneling probability increases, and the gate-leakage current density increases \cite{green2001review}.

In a classical model, an electron whose energy is lower than the oxide barrier cannot cross it. However, in quantum mechanics, the electron wavefunction extends into the classically forbidden region, producing a nonzero probability of transmission \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}. The applied positive gate voltage creates a potential difference between the gate and the semiconductor, part of which appears as a voltage drop \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\) across the oxide. Assuming that the electric field inside the oxide is approximately uniform, its magnitude is
\[
F_{\mathrm{ox}}\approx\frac{V_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}.
\]
Because electrons carry negative charge, an increase in electrical potential corresponds to a decrease in their electrostatic potential energy. The applied electric field therefore tilts the oxide potential barrier, allowing it to be approximated as trapezoidal rather than rectangular \cite{simmons1963}.

Research on gate-oxide tunneling developed from general insulating-film transport theories into models designed for MOS structures. Simmons (1963) derived a generalized expression for tunneling current density through a thin insulating film under an applied voltage, providing a basis for relating barrier transmission to measurable current \cite{simmons1963}. Lenzlinger and Snow (1969) later observed Fowler--Nordheim behavior in thermally grown silicon dioxide over more than five orders of magnitude in current and used the current--field relationship to estimate oxide tunneling parameters \cite{lenzlinger1969}. Their results connected general barrier theory with electrical measurements of SiO$_2$ and included an estimate of the electron effective mass within the oxide. However, Fowler--Nordheim tunneling mainly describes high-field transmission through part of a strongly tilted barrier, whereas direct tunneling through the full barrier becomes increasingly important as the oxide is made thinner.

As gate oxides entered the nanometer range, research shifted toward direct tunneling and its effect on oxide scaling. Schuegraf, King, and Hu (1992) identified direct tunneling as the dominant leakage mechanism in ultrathin SiO$_2$ and examined how it restricts further reduction in oxide thickness \cite{schuegraf1992}. Depas et al. (1995) analyzed current--voltage measurements from poly-Si/SiO$_2$/Si structures with oxide thicknesses between 3 and 6 nm \cite{depas1995}. By extending an analytical Fowler--Nordheim expression, they found that direct tunneling became important below approximately 4.5 nm. Their WKB model also accounted for band bending in the silicon substrate and poly-Si gate. Using the oxide electron effective mass as a fitting parameter, they showed that the model could describe both direct and Fowler--Nordheim currents quantitatively. Green et al. (2001) later reviewed the physical and electrical limits of ultrathin SiO$_2$ and Si--O--N gate dielectrics, emphasizing the practical importance of understanding how leakage current depends on oxide thickness \cite{green2001review}.

We will establish a leakage model using a one-dimensional trapezoidal potential barrier and the Wentzel--Kramers--Brillouin (WKB) approximation. The resulting thickness dependence will be evaluated using published measurements of gate-leakage current density \(J_G\) as a function of oxide thickness \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) \cite{green1999limits}. In particular, we will test whether the experimental data support the approximately exponential thickness dependence predicted by the model and identify where the simplified relationship departs systematically from the measurements.

\section{Theoretical Background}

\subsection{MOSFET Structure and Gate-Oxide Leakage}

The enhancement-mode n-channel metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistor (nMOSFET) considered in this paper consists of four terminals: the gate, source, drain, and body. The source and drain are heavily doped \(n^+\) regions formed within a p-type body, while the gate is electrically separated from the semiconductor by a thin oxide layer. In this paper, the source and body are grounded, so \(V_S=V_B=0\). Therefore, the gate-to-source voltage is \(V_{GS}=V_G-V_S=V_G\), and the drain-to-source voltage is \(V_{DS}=V_D-V_S=V_D\). These voltages have different functions: changing \(V_{GS}\) alters the vertical electric field across the gate stack and controls the charge distribution beneath the gate, whereas \(V_{DS}\) produces a lateral electric field that drives carriers through an established inversion channel. An inversion channel is an electron-rich conducting layer formed at the oxide--silicon interface when a sufficiently positive gate voltage causes electrons to become the dominant mobile carriers at the surface of the p-type body. Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation} illustrates this operation in four stages \cite[Secs.~2.3 and 3.1]{taur2009}.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(a), \(V_{GS}=0\) and \(V_{DS}=0\). The source--body and drain--body p--n junctions contain built-in depletion regions, but no gate-induced inversion channel connects the source and drain. Therefore, the enhancement-mode nMOSFET is in its off state.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(b), a positive gate voltage is applied such that \(0<V_{GS}<V_T\), where \(V_T\) is the threshold voltage. Positive charge on the gate produces a vertical electric field across the oxide. This field repels holes from the oxide--silicon interface and leaves negatively charged ionized acceptor atoms in the p-type body, forming a gate-induced depletion region. Electrons are also attracted toward the interface, but their concentration remains insufficient to form a continuous conducting channel.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(c), \(V_{GS}\) exceeds \(V_T\). The electron concentration at the oxide--silicon interface becomes sufficiently high to form a strong inversion layer. This electron-rich layer creates a conductive channel between the \(n^+\) source and drain. However, because \(V_{DS}=0\), there is no lateral electric field along the channel and therefore no net drain current \(I_D\).

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(d), \(V_{GS}>V_T\) is maintained while a positive drain-to-source voltage \(V_{DS}>0\) is applied. The resulting lateral electric field drives electrons through the inversion channel from the source to the drain. Conventional drain current \(I_D\) flows in the opposite direction, from the drain to the source. This source--drain current is the functional current used for switching and signal transmission. At the same time, the vertical electric field remains present across the gate oxide. Although the oxide suppresses classical carrier transport, a sufficiently thin oxide allows electrons to tunnel through the potential barrier, producing the parasitic gate-leakage current \(I_G\) \cite{green2001review}. The direction of this tunneling current depends on the applied bias and carrier-injection conditions and will be specified after the experimental dataset is introduced.

The oxide can be modeled as the dielectric in a capacitor. Its oxide capacitance per unit area  \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\)  is
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{ox}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}},
\end{equation}
where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide permittivity and \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide thickness \cite{taur2009}. The potential difference across the oxide is denoted by \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\), which is not generally identical to \(V_{GS}\). Reducing \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) increases \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\), thereby strengthening electrostatic control. However, under otherwise comparable material and bias conditions, the barrier width also decreases, increasing both the transmission probability \(T\) and the gate-leakage current density \(J_G\) \cite{green2001review}.

\section{Model Construction}

\subsection{Model Assumptions and Barrier Definition}

This model is constructed to determine the electron transmission probability \(T\). Electron motion is assumed to be one-dimensional and perpendicular to the oxide layer, with \(x\) representing this direction. The oxide thickness \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is therefore modeled as the width of the potential barrier. Assuming that the electric field is approximately uniform inside the oxide, the electron potential energy varies linearly with position, so the oxide barrier is approximated as trapezoidal:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta(x)\equiv U(x)-E
    =\Delta_0-\frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}x,
    \qquad 0\leq x\leq t_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-barrier}
\end{equation}

Here, \(x=0\) is the oxide--substrate interface and \(x=t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the gate--oxide interface. Positive \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is defined such that the electron potential energy decreases along the positive \(x\)-direction. The quantity
\(\Delta(x)=U(x)-E\) represents the local barrier energy relative to the electron energy \(E\), while
\(\Delta_0=U(0)-E\) is the barrier energy at the oxide--substrate interface.

Figure~\ref{fig:trapezoidal-model} illustrates this one-dimensional potential-energy profile. The applied oxide voltage tilts the barrier so that \(U(x)\) decreases approximately linearly across the oxide. The horizontal line represents the electron energy \(E\). In the trapezoidal case considered here, the barrier remains above \(E\) throughout the oxide, so the entire interval from \(x=0\) to \(x=t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is classically forbidden.

The oxide extends from the oxide--substrate interface at \(x=0\) to the gate--oxide interface at \(x=t_{\mathrm{ox}}\). Because \(U(x)>E\) throughout the oxide, the entire oxide constitutes a classically forbidden region.}
    \label{fig:trapezoidal-model}
\end{figure}

At the gate--oxide interface, the barrier energy is

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_1\equiv\Delta(t_{\mathrm{ox}})
    =\Delta_0-qV_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:gate-oxide-barrier}
\end{equation}

For the complete trapezoidal barrier shown in Figure~\ref{fig:trapezoidal-model}, the condition

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_1>0
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-condition}
\end{equation}

must be satisfied. This ensures that \(U(x)>E\) throughout the entire oxide. If \(\Delta_1\leq0\), the potential crosses the electron energy inside the oxide, producing a classical turning point. In that case, the classically forbidden region would terminate at the turning point rather than at \(x=t_{\mathrm{ox}}\).

\subsection{Derivation of the WKB Transmission Probability}
To determine the electron transmission probability \(T\) for motion along the \(x\)-direction, we follow the standard one-dimensional WKB treatment \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}\cite{townsend2010}, beginning with the time-independent Schr\"odinger equation.
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\frac{d^2\psi(x)}{dx^2}
    +U(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x),
    \label{eq:tise}
\end{equation}
where \(\psi(x)\) is the electron wavefunction, \(U(x)\) is the potential energy, \(E\) is the electron energy, \(m^*\) is the electron effective mass within the oxide, and \(\hbar\) is the reduced Planck constant.

As the potential energy \(U(x)\) changes with position \(x\), we cannot solve the Schr\"odinger equation by simply applying a plane-wave solution. Thus, we rewrite the wavefunction as the product of a slowly varying amplitude and a rapidly varying phase.
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x)=A(x)e^{i\phi(x)}.
    \label{eq:wkb-ansatz}
\end{equation}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-ansatz} into Equation~\eqref{eq:tise} and separating the result into real-part and imaginary-part equations gives
\begin{align}
    A''(x)-A(x)\left[\phi'(x)\right]^2
    &=-\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}[E-U(x)]A(x),
    \label{eq:wkb-real}\\
    2A'(x)\phi'(x)+A(x)\phi''(x)
    &=0.
    \label{eq:wkb-imag}
\end{align}

In the WKB approximation, we assume that the amplitude \(A(x)\) varies much more slowly than the phase \(\phi(x)\). Thus, the amplitude-curvature term \(A''(x)\) is much smaller than the phase term and can be neglected. Then, the real-part equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-real} gives the local phase gradient. Meanwhile, the imaginary-part equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-imag} gives the relationship between the amplitude and the local momentum.
\begin{align}
    \phi'(x)&\approx\pm\frac{p(x)}{\hbar},
    \label{eq:phase-gradient}\\
    2A'\phi'+A\phi''=0
    &\ \Longrightarrow\
    \frac{d}{dx}\left[A^2(x)\phi'(x)\right]=0
    \ \Longrightarrow\
    A(x)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}},
    \label{eq:wkb-amplitude}
\end{align}
where \(p(x)=\sqrt{2m^*[E-U(x)]}\) represents the local classical momentum in the classically allowed region.

Integrating Equation~\eqref{eq:phase-gradient} gives
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x)\approx
    \pm\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx',
    \label{eq:wkb-phase}
\end{equation}
where the constant phase has been absorbed into the coefficients. Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:wkb-phase} and \eqref{eq:wkb-amplitude} back into the wavefunction gives the allowed-region WKB solution
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}}
    \left[
        C_{+}\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx'\right)
        +C_{-}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx'\right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:allowed-wkb}
\end{equation}
When \(E>U(x)\), \(p(x)\) is real, so the two exponential terms are oscillatory. The region where \(E>U(x)\) is called the classically allowed region.

\subsection{WKB Solution in the Classically Forbidden Region}
Unlike the classically allowed region, the classically forbidden region is defined by \(U(x)>E\), so the classical kinetic energy \(E-U(x)\) is negative. Thus, \(p(x)\) is imaginary. We define the attenuation coefficient \(\kappa(x)\), which describes the inverse decay length inside the barrier, as
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(x)\equiv
    \frac{\sqrt{2m^*[U(x)-E]}}{\hbar},
    \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
so that \(p(x)=i\hbar\kappa(x)\).

After substituting \(p(x)=i\hbar\kappa(x)\) into the exponential term of the allowed-region solution,
\begin{equation}
    \exp\left[\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int p(x)\,dx\right]
    \longrightarrow
    \exp\left[\mp\int\kappa(x)\,dx\right],
    \label{eq:forbidden-exponential}
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}
    \left[
        C\exp\left(-\int^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right)
        +D\exp\left(+\int^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:forbidden-wkb}
\end{equation}
where constant phase factors have been absorbed into \(C\) and \(D\).
The first term is the exponentially decaying component and the second term is the exponentially growing component. 

To compare the two components, define
\begin{equation}
    S(x)\equiv\int_{x_1}^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx',
    \qquad
    K\equiv S(x_2)=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\kappa(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:barrier-action}
\end{equation}
where \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are the boundaries of the classically forbidden region. Equation~\eqref{eq:forbidden-wkb} can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}
    \left[Ce^{-S(x)}+De^{S(x)}\right].
    \label{eq:forbidden-action}
\end{equation}

From Equation~\eqref{eq:barrier-action}, \(S(x_1)=0\) and \(S(x_2)=K\). The standard WKB connection result therefore gives, to leading exponential order,
\[
    \frac{A_{\mathrm{trans}}}{A_{\mathrm{inc}}}\sim e^{-K},
\]
up to non-exponential interface factors.

The transmission probability is the ratio of transmitted to incident probability current. Retaining only its leading exponential dependence gives the standard WKB transmission expression \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}:
\begin{align}
    T
    &\equiv\frac{j_{\mathrm{trans}}}{j_{\mathrm{inc}}}
    \approx e^{-2K}
    =\exp\left[
        -2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\kappa(x)\,dx
    \right]
    \label{eq:wkb-transmission-kappa}\\
    &=\exp\left[
        -\frac{2}{\hbar}
        \int_{x_1}^{x_2}
        \sqrt{2m^*[U(x)-E]}\,dx
    \right].
    \label{eq:wkb-transmission}
\end{align}
\subsection{Application to a Trapezoidal Potential Barrier}
To approximate the electron transmission probability, we then evaluate the integral over the classically forbidden region. According to Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-condition}, \(\Delta_1>0\), so \(U(x)>E\) throughout the oxide. Therefore, the forbidden interval contains the whole oxide:
\begin{equation}
    x_1=0,
    \qquad
    x_2=t_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:oxide-forbidden-interval}
\end{equation}
Substituting these boundaries and Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-barrier} into Equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-transmission} gives
\begin{equation}
    T\approx
    \exp\left[
        -\frac{2\sqrt{2m^*}}{\hbar}
        \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}
        \sqrt{
            \Delta_0-
            \frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}x
        }\,dx
    \right],
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-wkb-integral}
\end{equation}
where \(m^*\) is assumed to remain constant throughout the oxide.

Using the substitution
\begin{equation}
    u=\Delta_0-\frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}x,
    \qquad
    du=-\frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}\,dx,
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-substitution}
\end{equation}
the integral becomes
\begin{align}
    \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}
    \sqrt{
        \Delta_0-
        \frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}x
    }\,dx
    &=
    \frac{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}
    \int_{\Delta_1}^{\Delta_0}u^{1/2}\,du
    \notag\\
    &=
    \frac{2t_{\mathrm{ox}}}{3qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}
    \left(
        \Delta_0^{3/2}-\Delta_1^{3/2}
    \right).
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-integral}
\end{align}
The negative sign in Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-substitution} is removed by reversing the integration limits from \(\Delta_0\) to \(\Delta_1\), giving limits from \(\Delta_1\) to \(\Delta_0\). Then, substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-integral} into the WKB approximation for \(T\) gives
\begin{equation}
    T\approx
    \exp\left[
        -\frac{4t_{\mathrm{ox}}\sqrt{2m^*}}
        {3\hbar qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}
        \left(
            \Delta_0^{3/2}-\Delta_1^{3/2}
        \right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-transmission}
\end{equation}
As a consistency check, Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-transmission} gives \(T\to1\) as \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\to0\) and \(T\to0\) as \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\to\infty\), with the other barrier parameters held constant. Although the WKB approximation is not valid at exactly zero barrier width, these formal limits agree with physical intuition.

When \(\Delta_0\), \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\), and \(m^*\) are held constant, Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-transmission} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \ln T\approx-\beta t_{\mathrm{ox}},
    \label{eq:log-transmission-thickness}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \beta\equiv
    \frac{4\sqrt{2m^*}}
    {3\hbar qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}
    \left(
        \Delta_0^{3/2}-\Delta_1^{3/2}
    \right)>0.
    \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Thus, Equation~\eqref{eq:log-transmission-thickness} predicts that the electron transmission probability \(T\) increases exponentially as the oxide thickness decreases. In addition, increasing \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\), the positive potential difference across the oxide, lowers \(\beta\) and thus increases the transmission probability \(T\), provided that \(0<qV_{\mathrm{ox}}<\Delta_0\) and that \(\Delta_0\) and \(m^*\) remain constant.

\subsection{Connection between Transmission Probability and Gate Current Density}

The magnitude of the gate current density \(\left|J_G\right|\) can be related to the electron transmission probability:
\begin{align}
    \left|J_G\right|
    &\equiv \frac{\left|Q_{\mathrm{trans}}\right|}{\mathcal{A}\Delta t}
    =\frac{qN_{\mathrm{trans}}}{\mathcal{A}\Delta t}
    \notag\\
    &=\frac{qTN_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\mathcal{A}\Delta t}
    =q\Phi_{\mathrm{inc}}T,
    \label{eq:gate-current-flux}
\end{align}
Here, \(\left|Q_{\mathrm{trans}}\right|\) is the magnitude of the charge transmitted through the oxide during the time interval \(\Delta t\), and \(N_{\mathrm{trans}}\) is the corresponding number of transmitted electrons. The quantity \(N_{\mathrm{inc}}\) is the number of electrons incident on the oxide--substrate interface over the area \(\mathcal{A}\) during the same interval, so \(N_{\mathrm{trans}}=TN_{\mathrm{inc}}\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{inc}}\equiv N_{\mathrm{inc}}/(\mathcal{A}\Delta t)\). Reverse-direction tunneling is assumed to be negligible. For the purpose of investigating the leading dependence on oxide thickness, the incident-electron supply is assumed to remain approximately constant across the samples. Then, we can write \(\left|J_G\right|\) as
\begin{equation}
    \left|J_G\right|\approx J_0T,
    \label{eq:gate-current-proportional}
\end{equation}
where \(J_0\) absorbs the remaining non-exponential factors. Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:gate-current-proportional} into Equation~\eqref{eq:log-transmission-thickness} gives
\begin{equation}
    \ln \left(\frac{\left|J_G\right|}{J_0}\right)\approx-\beta t_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:log-gate-current-thickness}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Data Digitization and Processing}

Because the numerical values are presented graphically rather than in a table, we extracted the coordinates from the original published figure using WebPlotDigitizer \cite{rohatgiWebPlotDigitizer}. The horizontal axis was calibrated as a linear scale from \(0.4\) to \(3.2~\mathrm{nm}\), while the vertical axis was calibrated as a logarithmic scale from \(10^{-10}\) to \(10^{5}~\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}\). We then manually selected the centers of the seven circular data markers. This extraction was repeated independently three times to estimate the uncertainty associated with axis calibration and manual point selection. Figure~\ref{fig:reconstructed-gate-leakage} displays the resulting mean coordinates.

For each data point \(i\), the three oxide-thickness readings were combined using their arithmetic mean. Because \(\left|J_G\right|\) spans more than twelve orders of magnitude, the current-density readings were first transformed as
\begin{equation}
    y_{i,r}
    \equiv
    \ln\left(
        \frac{\left|J_{G,i,r}\right|}{J_{\mathrm{ref}}}
    \right),
    \qquad
    J_{\mathrm{ref}}=1~\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}},
    \label{eq:digitized-log-current}
\end{equation}
where \(r\) identifies the digitization run and \(J_{\mathrm{ref}}\) makes the argument of the logarithm dimensionless. With \(n=3\) runs, the mean values were calculated using
\begin{equation}
    \bar{t}_i=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}t_{i,r},
    \qquad
    \bar{y}_i=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}y_{i,r}.
    \label{eq:digitized-means}
\end{equation}
The repeatability of the digitization procedure was represented by the sample standard deviations
\begin{equation}
    s_{t,i}
    =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(t_{i,r}-\bar{t}_i\right)^2},
    \qquad
    s_{y,i}
    =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(y_{i,r}-\bar{y}_i\right)^2}.
    \label{eq:digitized-standard-deviations}
\end{equation}
Converting \(\bar{y}_i\) back to the original current-density scale gives
\begin{equation}
    \left|J_G\right|_{\mathrm{geom},i}
    =J_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{\bar{y}_i},
    \label{eq:digitized-geometric-mean}
\end{equation}
which is the geometric mean of the three extracted current-density values.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Comparison with Experimental Gate Leakage}

Equation~\eqref{eq:log-gate-current-thickness} predicts that, if \(\beta\) and \(J_0\) remain approximately constant, \(\ln |J_G|\) should decrease linearly as \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) increases. The experimental regression produced \(R^2=0.9743\), indicating a strong approximately linear relationship between \(\ln |J_G|\) and \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) over the investigated thickness range. Therefore, the experimental data support the leading exponential dependence of gate current density on oxide thickness predicted by the WKB model. However, this agreement only supports the functional form of the prediction. It does not independently validate the numerical value of the WKB exponent, because the device-specific values of \(\Delta_0\), \(m^*\), and \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\) required to calculate the theoretical value of \(\beta\) are not available for this dataset.

The fitted logarithmic slope also quantifies the sensitivity of gate leakage to oxide thickness. The thickness increase required to reduce the gate current density by exactly one order of magnitude is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t_{\mathrm{decade}}
    =
    \frac{\ln 10}{\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}}
    =
    0.197~\mathrm{nm}.
\end{equation}
Thus, increasing the oxide thickness by approximately \(0.197~\mathrm{nm}\) corresponds to a tenfold decrease in gate current density. This result demonstrates why very small variations in an ultrathin oxide can produce large changes in gate leakage: the barrier width appears in the exponent of the WKB transmission probability.
 
Despite the high value of \(R^2\), the linear model does not describe all seven data points perfectly. To examine whether the deviations from the fitted line are randomly distributed, we define the residual for point \(i\) as
\begin{equation}
    r_i
    \equiv
    \bar{y}_i-\hat{y}_i
    =
    \bar{y}_i-
    \left(
        a-\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}\bar{t}_i
    \right),
    \label{eq:experimental-residual}
\end{equation}
where \(\bar{y}_i\) is the observed logarithmic current density and \(\hat{y}_i\) is the value predicted by Equation~\eqref{eq:experimental-fit-result}. A positive residual therefore indicates that the observed value lies above the fitted line, whereas a negative residual indicates that it lies below the line.

Thus, the constant-slope model tends to underestimate \(\ln|J_G|\) near the lower and upper ends of the investigated thickness range and overestimate it over the central range. The absolute residuals, approximately \(0.383\) to \(1.885\), are also substantially larger than the digitization standard deviations of approximately \(0.020\) to \(0.059\). This comparison suggests that the observed pattern is unlikely to result solely from the repeatability of manual point selection. However, the digitization standard deviations do not include the experimental uncertainty of the original measurements. In addition, the dataset contains only seven points, and the thickest-oxide point is separated from the other thicknesses by more than \(1~\mathrm{nm}\). Therefore, the residual plot provides evidence of possible systematic curvature, but it does not by itself establish the functional form or physical cause of the deviation.


\subsection{Voltage-Dependent Validation of the Trapezoidal-Barrier Model}

The preceding comparison with the Green dataset tests the predicted exponential dependence on oxide thickness, but this functional form is not unique to a trapezoidal barrier. A fixed rectangular barrier also gives an expression of the form \(\ln |J_G|=a-\beta t_{\mathrm{ox}}\). For a rectangular barrier, the two endpoint barrier energies are equal, so \(\Delta_0=\Delta_1\). If this common barrier height and the effective mass remain constant, the logarithmic decay coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{\mathrm{rect}}
    =
    \frac{2\sqrt{2m^*\Delta_0}}{\hbar},
    \label{eq:rectangular-beta}
\end{equation}
which is independent of the applied voltage. By contrast, the trapezoidal barrier becomes more strongly tilted as the magnitude of the oxide voltage increases, so Equation~\eqref{eq:beta} predicts that the decay coefficient decreases. The voltage dependence of the experimental slope therefore provides a second test that can distinguish the trapezoidal model from a fixed rectangular one.

Khairurrijal et al. reported measured direct-tunneling current densities for phosphorus-doped \(n^+\) poly-Si-gate MOS capacitors with oxide thicknesses of \(1.65\), \(2.17\), \(2.51\), \(2.80\), \(3.50\), and \(3.90~\mathrm{nm}\) \cite{khairurrijal2000}. Their measurements were made at room temperature under negative gate bias, and the oxide voltage was obtained from capacitance--voltage measurements after corrections for the flat-band voltage and substrate surface potential. Because this bias reverses the injection direction relative to the positive-bias nMOSFET convention used earlier, we use the magnitude \(\lvert V_{\mathrm{ox}}\rvert\). Reversing the coordinate direction does not change the WKB action across the same barrier.

Only the measured markers in Figure~3 of Khairurrijal et al. were used; the authors' calculated curves were not digitized. At the nominal oxide-voltage magnitudes \(1.0\), \(1.5\), \(2.0\), and \(2.5~\mathrm{V}\), the six current-density values were independently digitized three times using WebPlotDigitizer \cite{rohatgiWebPlotDigitizer}. Since equal vertical displacements on the published logarithmic axis represent equal multiplicative changes in current density, each reading was first transformed as \(\ln(|J|/J_{\mathrm{ref}})\). The three logarithmic values were then averaged. This procedure is equivalent to taking the geometric mean of the three current-density readings and is consistent with the variable used in the subsequent regression. At each fixed voltage, an ordinary least-squares regression was then performed in the form
\begin{equation}
    \overline{\ln\left(|J|/J_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)}
    =
    a(V_{\mathrm{ox}})
    -
    \beta_{\mathrm{exp}}(V_{\mathrm{ox}})t_{\mathrm{ox}},
    \qquad
    J_{\mathrm{ref}}=1~\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}.
    \label{eq:khairurrijal-fixed-voltage-fit}
\end{equation}
A separate intercept \(a(V_{\mathrm{ox}})\) was allowed at each voltage because the incident-electron supply and other non-exponential factors can change with voltage. The comparison therefore focuses on the fitted slope \(\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}\).

The \(R^2\) values between \(0.9844\) and \(0.9872\) show that the approximate exponential thickness dependence is present at all four voltages. More importantly, \(\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}\) decreases monotonically from \(8.331\) to \(7.522~\mathrm{nm^{-1}}\) as \(\lvert V_{\mathrm{ox}}\rvert\) increases from \(1.0\) to \(2.5~\mathrm{V}\). This voltage-dependent change, rather than the high \(R^2\) values alone, is the feature that can distinguish the two barrier models.

An absolute comparison between the experimental and theoretical decay coefficients would require an independently known oxide effective mass. In the Khairurrijal model, however, the oxide effective mass and the gate Fermi energy were fitting parameters, and the fitted effective mass also varied with oxide thickness \cite{khairurrijal2000}. Normalization does not make these thickness-dependent masses equal, but it can reduce their influence under additional assumptions. We therefore normalize each decay coefficient to the value at
\[
    V_{\mathrm{ref}}=1.5~\mathrm{V}.
\]
The value \(1.5~\mathrm{V}\) was selected because it is an interior point of the investigated voltage range and avoids using the lowest-voltage point as the normalization reference. The choice is a normalization convention rather than a fitted parameter: choosing another voltage common to all six thicknesses would rescale both sets of ratios but would not change whether the decay coefficient increases or decreases with voltage. The experimental and theoretical ratios are defined as
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{exp}}(V)
    =
    \frac{\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}(V)}
    {\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}(V_{\mathrm{ref}})},
    \qquad
    R_{\mathrm{trap}}(V)
    =
    \frac{\beta_{\mathrm{trap}}(V)}
    {\beta_{\mathrm{trap}}(V_{\mathrm{ref}})}.
    \label{eq:normalized-decay-coefficients}
\end{equation}
In the simplified theoretical expression, a single voltage-independent effective mass appears as the common factor \(\sqrt{m^*}\), so this factor cancels from \(R_{\mathrm{trap}}\). The experimental value \(\beta_{\mathrm{exp}}\), however, is an effective slope obtained by fitting devices with different oxide thicknesses and different fitted effective masses. Its normalization is therefore interpretable only if the effective mass associated with each device remains approximately independent of voltage and the mass-related thickness dependence affects all four fixed-voltage regressions in a similar way. Consequently, the normalized comparison tests the predicted voltage trend rather than independently validating the absolute WKB exponent.

Under the negative gate bias used by Khairurrijal et al., the barrier orientation is reversed relative to the positive-bias case used in the model derivation. With the original coordinate definitions retained, the gate-side barrier \(\Delta_1\) is therefore the higher endpoint, while the substrate-side barrier satisfies
\[
    \Delta_0=\Delta_1-q\lvert V_{\mathrm{ox}}\rvert.
\]
Interchanging the high and low endpoints does not change the WKB action, so the theoretical ratios can still be evaluated from Equation~\eqref{eq:beta} using the voltage magnitude. Khairurrijal et al. used a measured conduction-band alignment of \(3.34~\mathrm{eV}\) and independently estimated central values of the gate Fermi energy of \(0.05\) and \(0.20~\mathrm{eV}\) \cite{khairurrijal2000}. Since their reported gate-side barrier height is \(3.34~\mathrm{eV}-E_F\), these two estimates give \(\Delta_1=3.29\) and \(3.14~\mathrm{eV}\), respectively. The paper does not identify either central estimate as uniquely correct, so both values were used to evaluate the normalized trapezoidal prediction. The region between the resulting predictions is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:normalized-decay-coefficient} as a barrier-height sensitivity range; it is not a statistical confidence interval. The complete-trapezoid condition \(q\lvert V_{\mathrm{ox}}\rvert<\Delta_1\) remains satisfied at all four selected voltages.

For all six oxides, the experimental ratios are \(1.0255\), \(1.0000\), \(0.9676\), and \(0.9258\), whereas a fixed rectangular barrier predicts \(R_{\mathrm{rect}}=1\) at every voltage. The experimental change has the same direction as the trapezoidal prediction, although its magnitude is smaller. Using the midpoint \(\Delta_1=3.215~\mathrm{eV}\), the descriptive root-mean-square difference between the four experimental ratios and the trapezoidal prediction is \(0.029\), compared with \(0.042\) for the fixed rectangular prediction.

Khairurrijal et al. reported that the oxides thinner than \(3~\mathrm{nm}\) were grown in \(2\%\) O\(_2\) diluted with N\(_2\), whereas those thicker than \(3~\mathrm{nm}\) were grown in \(100\%\) dry O\(_2\) \cite{khairurrijal2000}. Because this fabrication difference could affect the thickness comparison, the regression was repeated using only the \(1.65\), \(2.17\), \(2.51\), and \(2.80~\mathrm{nm}\) oxides. This subset was defined from the reported growth conditions rather than from the regression residuals. It gives normalized coefficients of \(1.0361\), \(1.0000\), \(0.9520\), and \(0.8852\). For the same midpoint barrier height, the root-mean-square difference is \(0.010\) from the trapezoidal prediction and \(0.065\) from the rectangular prediction. The voltage-dependent decrease therefore remains when the fabrication method is held more nearly constant, although the smaller subset contains only four thicknesses.

These results do not prove that the physical barrier is exactly trapezoidal. The original point-by-point experimental uncertainties are unavailable, so the small spread among repeated digitizations cannot be interpreted as the total measurement uncertainty. In addition, Khairurrijal et al. found a thickness-dependent fitted effective mass and obtained better agreement using a nonparabolic oxide dispersion relation than using a parabolic one \cite{khairurrijal2000}. Their devices were also MOS capacitors under negative gate bias rather than the positively biased nMOSFET configuration introduced in this paper. Nevertheless, the observed decrease of the experimental decay coefficient with oxide voltage is more consistent with the trapezoidal-barrier prediction than with a fixed rectangular barrier. The Green dataset therefore provides the first validation of the approximate exponential thickness dependence, while the Khairurrijal dataset supplies a complementary second validation based on the voltage dependence of that exponential decay.
