\section{Introduction}
Metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are widely used as electronic switches because the voltage applied to the gate controls the conductivity of the channel. 
%%这里衔接太生硬，给一点context，稍微解释一下
An oxide layer is placed between the gate and the semiconductor channel to electrically insulate the gate from the silicon channel while allowing electrostatic control. Reducing the oxide thickness increases the oxide capacitance, allowing the gate voltage to control the channel charge more effectively. However, at the nanometer scale, a thinner oxide also means a narrower potential barrier. Thus, the tunneling probability increases, and the gate-leakage current density increases \cite{green2001review}.

In a classical model, an electron with lower energy than the oxide barrier height cannot cross the oxide barrier. However, in quantum mechanic situation, its wavefunction extends into the classically forbidden region, producing a nonzero probability of transmission \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}.
%%Apply V to gate: E across ocide layer
Since the oxide electric field makes the electron potential energy vary with position, a trapezoidal barrier is used \cite{simmons1963}.

We will establish a model of leakage using a one-dimensional trapezoidal potential barrier by applying the Wentzel--Kramers--Brillouin (WKB) approximation and computational model with Python, evaluate the model with published \(J_G\)--\(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) %%解释这两个东西是什么
data \cite{green1999limits} by comparing the dependence of theoretical tunneling probability and experimental gate-leakage current density on oxide thickness, and estimate leakage-related electrical power density before discussing its possible implications for energy efficiency, thermal stability, and reliability \cite{roy2003,skadron2004}.

\section{Theoretical Background}

\subsection{MOSFET Structure and Gate-Oxide Leakage}

The enhancement-mode n-channel metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistor (nMOSFET) considered in this paper consists of four terminals: the gate, source, drain, and body. The source and drain are heavily doped \(n^+\) regions formed within a p-type body, while the gate is electrically separated from the semiconductor by a thin oxide layer. In this paper, the source and body are grounded, so \(V_S=V_B=0\). Therefore, the gate-to-source voltage is \(V_{GS}=V_G-V_S=V_G\), and the drain-to-source voltage is \(V_{DS}=V_D-V_S=V_D\). These voltages have different functions: changing \(V_{GS}\) alters the vertical electric field across the gate stack and controls the charge distribution beneath the gate, whereas \(V_{DS}\) produces a lateral electric field that drives carriers through an established inversion channel. An inversion channel is an electron-rich conducting layer formed at the oxide--silicon interface when a sufficiently positive gate voltage causes electrons to become the dominant mobile carriers at the surface of the p-type body. Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation} illustrates this operation in four stages \cite[Secs.~2.3 and 3.1]{taur2009}.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(a), \(V_{GS}=0\) and \(V_{DS}=0\). The source--body and drain--body p--n junctions contain built-in depletion regions, but no gate-induced inversion channel connects the source and drain. Therefore, the enhancement-mode nMOSFET is in its off state.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(b), a positive gate voltage is applied such that \(0<V_{GS}<V_T\), where \(V_T\) is the threshold voltage. Positive charge on the gate produces a vertical electric field across the oxide. This field repels holes from the oxide--silicon interface and leaves negatively charged ionized acceptor atoms in the p-type body, forming a gate-induced depletion region. Electrons are also attracted toward the interface, but their concentration remains insufficient to form a continuous conducting channel.

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(c), \(V_{GS}\) exceeds \(V_T\). The electron concentration at the oxide--silicon interface becomes sufficiently high to form a strong inversion layer. This electron-rich layer creates a conductive channel between the \(n^+\) source and drain. However, because \(V_{DS}=0\), there is no lateral electric field along the channel and therefore no net drain current \(I_D\).

In Figure~\ref{fig:nmos-operation}(d), \(V_{GS}>V_T\) is maintained while a positive drain-to-source voltage \(V_{DS}>0\) is applied. The resulting lateral electric field drives electrons through the inversion channel from the source to the drain. Conventional drain current \(I_D\) flows in the opposite direction, from the drain to the source. This source--drain current is the functional current used for switching and signal transmission. At the same time, the vertical electric field remains present across the gate oxide. Although the oxide suppresses classical carrier transport, a sufficiently thin oxide allows electrons to tunnel through the potential barrier, producing the parasitic gate-leakage current \(I_G\) \cite{green2001review}. The direction of this tunneling current depends on the applied bias and carrier-injection conditions and will be specified after the experimental dataset is introduced.

The oxide can be modeled as the dielectric in a capacitor. Its oxide capacitance per unit area  \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\)  is
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{ox}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}},
\end{equation}
where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide permittivity and \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide thickness \cite{taur2009}. The potential difference across the oxide is denoted by \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\), which is not generally identical to \(V_{GS}\). Reducing \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) increases \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\), thereby strengthening electrostatic control. However, under otherwise comparable material and bias conditions, the barrier width also decreases, increasing both the transmission probability \(T\) and the gate-leakage current density \(J_G\) \cite{green2001review}.

\section{Model Construction}

\subsection{Model Assumptions and Barrier Definition}
This model is constructed to determine the electron transmission probability(T). We assume that electron always move along the direction perpendicular to oxide layer linearly(1D), and we use x to show this direction. In this model, oxide thickness(tox) will be modeled as the potential barrier. Assuming the electric field is constant inside the oxide, we will apply trapezoidal barrier model to MOSFET, our research object, because electron potential energy linearly changes along with x.  
\begin{equation}
    \Delta(x)\equiv U(x)-E
    =\Delta_0-\frac{qV_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}}x,
    \qquad 0\leq x\leq t_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-barrier}
\end{equation}
Here, \(x=0\) is taken at the electron-injection interface, and positive \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is defined such that the electron potential energy decreases along the \(+x\)-direction. In Equation~\eqref{eq:trapezoidal-barrier}, \(\Delta(x)=U(x)-E\) is the effective barrier energy that the electron must overcome at position \(x\); \(\Delta_0=U(0)-E\) is the barrier energy at the oxide entrance; \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the voltage drop across the oxide; \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide thickness; and \(q\) is the magnitude of the elementary charge.

At the oxide exit, the barrier energy is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_1\equiv\Delta(t_{\mathrm{ox}})
    =\Delta_0-qV_{\mathrm{ox}}.
    \label{eq:exit-barrier}
\end{equation}
The complete trapezoidal-barrier model therefore requires
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_1>0,
    \label{eq:trapezoidal-condition}
\end{equation}
so that \(U(x)>E\) throughout the oxide. If \(\Delta_1\leq 0\), a turning point occurs inside the oxide and the upper limit of the forbidden-region integral must be changed from \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) to that turning point.
\subsection{Derivation of the WKB Transmission Probability}
To determine the electron transmission probability(T) along x-direction motion, we will start from one dimensional time-independent  Schrodinger equation.
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\frac{d^2\psi(x)}{dx^2}
    +U(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x).
    \label{eq:tise}
\end{equation}
where \(\psi(x)\) is the electron wavefunction, \(U(x)\) is the potential energy, \(E\) is the electron energy, and \(m^*\) is the electron effective mass within the oxide.

As the potential energy (U(x)) changes with the different position(x), we cannot solve Schrodinger equation by simply apply plane-wave solution. Thus, we rewrite the wavefunction into the multiply of slowly change amplitude and fast change phase.
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x)=A(x)e^{i\phi(x)}.
    \label{eq:wkb-ansatz}
\end{equation}
Substitute Equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-ansatz} in to Equation~\eqref{eq:tise}, and separate the equation into real-part and imaginary-part equations: 
\begin{align}
    A''(x)-A(x)\left[\phi'(x)\right]^2
    &=-\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}[E-U(x)]A(x),
    \label{eq:wkb-real}\\
    2A'(x)\phi'(x)+A(x)\phi''(x)
    &=0.
    \label{eq:wkb-imag}
\end{align}

In WKB approximation, we assume that amplitude change (A(x)) changes much slower than phase(\(\phi(x)\)) changes. Thus, the amplitude curvature(A''(x)) term is much smaller than phase term and is negligible. Then, through real-part equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-real} we can obtain local phase gradient. Meanwhile, the imaginary-part equation~\eqref{eq:wkb-imag} can show the relationship between amplitude and local momentum.
\begin{align}
    \phi'(x)&\approx\pm\frac{p(x)}{\hbar},
    \label{eq:phase-gradient}\\
    2A'\phi'+A\phi''=0
    &\ \Longrightarrow\
    \frac{d}{dx}\left[A^2(x)\phi'(x)\right]=0
    \ \Longrightarrow\
    A(x)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}}.
    \label{eq:wkb-amplitude}
\end{align}
where \(p(x)=\sqrt{2m^*[E-U(x)]}\),
with p(x) represents the local classical momentum in allowed region.

Integrating Equation~\eqref{eq:phase-gradient} gives
\begin{equation}
    \phi(x)\approx
    \pm\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx',
    \label{eq:wkb-phase}
\end{equation}
where the constant phase has been absorbed into the coefficients. Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:wkb-phase} and \eqref{eq:wkb-amplitude} back into the wavefunction gives the allowed-region WKB solution
\begin{equation}
    \psi(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}}
    \left[
        C_{+}\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx'\right)
        +C_{-}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^{x}p(x')\,dx'\right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:allowed-wkb}
\end{equation}
When E>U(x), p(x) is a real number, so the two exponential terms are oscillatory. The region where E>U(x) is called classically allowed region.

\subsection{WKB Solution in the Classically Forbidden Region}
Different from classically allowed region, classically forbbiden region is where U(x)>E, so the classical kinetic energy E-U(x) will be negative. Thus, p(x) will become imaginary quantity. We define the decay constant describing the decay speed inside the barrier \(\kappa(x)\) as \begin{equation}
    \kappa(x)\equiv
    \frac{\sqrt{2m^*[U(x)-E]}}{\hbar}.
    \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation},
so, \(p(x)=i\hbar\kappa(x)\).

After substitute \(p(x)=i\hbar\kappa(x)\) into the exponential term of allowed-region solution, \begin{equation}
    \exp\left[\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int p(x)\,dx\right]
    \longrightarrow
    \exp\left[\mp\int\kappa(x)\,dx\right].
    \label{eq:forbidden-exponential}
\end{equation}, we get \begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}
    \left[
        C\exp\left(-\int^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right)
        +D\exp\left(+\int^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx'\right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:forbidden-wkb}
\end{equation}
where constant phase factors have been absorbed into \(C\) and \(D\).
The first term is the exponentially decaying component and the second term is the exponentially growing component. 

To retain both components while determining their relative importance, define
\begin{equation}
    S(x)\equiv\int_{x_1}^{x}\kappa(x')\,dx',
    \qquad
    K\equiv S(x_2)=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\kappa(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:barrier-action}
\end{equation}
where \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are the boundaries of the classically forbidden interval. Equation~\eqref{eq:forbidden-wkb} can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{B}}(x)\approx
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}
    \left[Ce^{-S(x)}+De^{S(x)}\right].
    \label{eq:forbidden-action}
\end{equation}
At \(x=x_2\), the decreasing and increasing components are proportional to \(Ce^{-K}\) and \(De^{K}\), respectively. If no wave is incident from the right, the allowed region beyond the barrier contains only the transmitted wave,
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{III}}(x)=t\,e^{ik_2(x-x_2)}.
    \label{eq:transmitted-wave}
\end{equation}
Matching the wavefunction and the appropriate flux-continuity condition at \(x_2\) gives, to leading exponential order,
\begin{equation}
    Ce^{-K}\sim t,
    \qquad
    De^{K}\sim t,
    \label{eq:right-boundary-matching}
\end{equation}
where \(\sim\) suppresses non-exponential factors arising from the wave vectors, effective masses, and interface matching. Dividing these relations gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{D}{C}\sim e^{-2K}.
    \label{eq:growing-coefficient-suppression}
\end{equation}
Therefore, when \(K\gg 1\), the coefficient of the increasing component is exponentially smaller than that of the decreasing component at the barrier entrance. This does not mean that the increasing component is identically zero throughout a finite barrier; near \(x_2\), the two contributions can be of comparable order.

At \(x=x_1\), \(S(x_1)=0\). Since \(D/C\ll 1\), the wavefunction at the entrance is dominated by \(C\), while Equation~\eqref{eq:right-boundary-matching} gives \(t\sim Ce^{-K}\). Thus, up to non-exponential interface factors,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{t}{A_{\mathrm{inc}}}\sim e^{-K},
    \label{eq:transmission-amplitude}
\end{equation}
where \(A_{\mathrm{inc}}\) is the incident-wave amplitude. The transmission probability is defined as the ratio of transmitted to incident probability current:
\begin{equation}
    T\equiv\frac{j_{\mathrm{trans}}}{j_{\mathrm{inc}}}
    \approx\mathcal{P}e^{-2K},
    \label{eq:transmission-prefactor}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{P}\) is a non-exponential prefactor determined by the interface matching, carrier velocities, and effective masses. If only the leading exponential dependence is retained,
\begin{align}
    T
    &\approx e^{-2K}
    =\exp\left[-2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\kappa(x)\,dx\right]
    \label{eq:wkb-transmission-kappa}\\
    &=\exp\left[
        -\frac{2}{\hbar}
        \int_{x_1}^{x_2}
        \sqrt{2m^*[U(x)-E]}\,dx
    \right].
    \label{eq:wkb-transmission}
\end{align}
