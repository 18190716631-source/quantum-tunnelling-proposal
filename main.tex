\section{Introduction}
Metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are widely used as electronic switches because the voltage applied to the gate controls the conductivity of the channel. Oxide layer is placed between the gate and the silicon semiconductor to electrically insulating the gate from the silicon channel while allowing electrostatic control. Reducing the oxide thickness increases the oxide capacitance, allowing the gate voltage to control the channel charge more effectively.. However, in the nanometer scale, thinner oxide also means a narrower potential barrier, thus tunneling probability increases and gate leakage current density increases. In classical model, an electron with insufficient energy (greater than oxide barrier height) cannot cross the oxide barrier; quantum mechanically, its wavefunction extends into the classically forbidden region, producing a nonzero probability of transmission. Since oxide electric field make electron potential energy changes as well as position, trapezoidal/slanted barrier is used. This paper will establish a model of leakage using one-dimensional trapezoidal/slanted barrier and WKB approximation; test the model with published JG-tox data compared with theoretical tunneling probability and experimental gate-leakage current density with the change of  oxide thickness; and discuss how thermal implications affect the efficiency, thermal stability, and reliability.
