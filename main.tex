\section{Introduction}
Metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are widely used as electronic switches because the voltage applied to the gate controls the conductivity of the channel. An oxide layer is placed between the gate and the silicon semiconductor to electrically insulate the gate from the silicon channel while allowing electrostatic control. Reducing the oxide thickness increases the oxide capacitance, allowing the gate voltage to control the channel charge more effectively. However, at the nanometer scale, a thinner oxide also means a narrower potential barrier; thus, the tunneling probability increases, and the gate-leakage current density increases \cite{green2001review}.

In a classical model, an electron with insufficient energy (lower than the oxide barrier height) cannot cross the oxide barrier. Quantum mechanically, its wavefunction extends into the classically forbidden region, producing a nonzero probability of transmission \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}. Since the oxide electric field makes the electron potential energy vary with position, a trapezoidal/slanted barrier is used \cite{simmons1963}.

This paper will establish a model of leakage using a one-dimensional trapezoidal potential barrier, the WKB approximation, and Python; evaluate the model with published \(J_G\)--\(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) data \cite{green1999limits} by comparing the dependence of theoretical tunneling probability and experimental gate-leakage current density on oxide thickness; and discuss how leakage-related power dissipation affects energy efficiency, thermal stability, and reliability \cite{roy2003,skadron2004}.

The enhancement-mode nMOSFET is controlled by the gate. Gate voltage, VG, will create an electric field in the oxide. When gate-to-source voltage is positive, electric field leads electrons approach the oxide-silicon interface. If VGS does not reach the threshold voltage VT, the electrons approaching the interface are not sufficient to create a current channel; by contrast, if VGS greater than VT, inversion layer will form at the interface, creating an electron channel connecting the source and the drain. This current, called drain current (ID), is the functional current used or signal transmission and switching. In contrast, the gate-leakage current IG crossing the oxide is parasitic.
