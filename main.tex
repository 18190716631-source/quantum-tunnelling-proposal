\section{Introduction}
Metal--oxide--semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are widely used as electronic switches because the voltage applied to the gate controls the conductivity of the channel. An oxide layer is placed between the gate and the silicon semiconductor to electrically insulate the gate from the silicon channel while allowing electrostatic control. Reducing the oxide thickness increases the oxide capacitance, allowing the gate voltage to control the channel charge more effectively. However, at the nanometer scale, a thinner oxide also means a narrower potential barrier; thus, the tunneling probability increases, and the gate-leakage current density increases \cite{green2001review}.

In a classical model, an electron with insufficient energy (lower than the oxide barrier height) cannot cross the oxide barrier. Quantum mechanically, its wavefunction extends into the classically forbidden region, producing a nonzero probability of transmission \cite[Sec.~9.2]{griffiths2018}. Since the oxide electric field makes the electron potential energy vary with position, a trapezoidal/slanted barrier is used \cite{simmons1963}.

This paper will establish a model of leakage using a one-dimensional trapezoidal potential barrier, the WKB approximation, and Python; evaluate the model with published \(J_G\)--\(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) data \cite{green1999limits} by comparing the dependence of theoretical tunneling probability and experimental gate-leakage current density on oxide thickness; and discuss how leakage-related power dissipation affects energy efficiency, thermal stability, and reliability \cite{roy2003,skadron2004}.

\section{Theoretical Background}

\subsection{MOSFET Structure and Gate-Oxide Leakage}

Figure~\ref{fig:mosfet-reference} shows a cross-sectional diagram of a MOSFET. The gate can switch or modulate the current between the drain and source. The oxide beneath the gate acts as an electrical insulator between the gate and the semiconductor, although electron tunneling can still occur when the oxide is sufficiently thin. The source is the terminal where electrons enter the channel, whereas the drain is where electrons leave the semiconductor channel; the channel is the pathway for current flow. The enhancement-mode nMOSFET is controlled by the gate. Assume the source is grounded so VS=0 and VGS=VG. The gate voltage \(V_G\) creates an electric field in the oxide. When the gate-to-source voltage \(V_{GS}\) is positive, this electric field attracts electrons toward the oxide--silicon interface under normal nMOS bias, VD>VS. If \(V_{GS}\) does not reach the threshold voltage \(V_T\), the electron concentration near the interface is insufficient to create a strong inversion channel. By contrast, if \(V_{GS}>V_T\), an inversion layer forms at the interface, creating an electron channel that connects the source and drain. Once the inversion channel has formed, a nonzero drain-to-source voltage \(V_{DS}\) produces a net drain current \(I_D\) through the channel. The drain current \(I_D\) is the functional current used for signal transmission and switching. In contrast, the gate-leakage current \(I_G\), which crosses the oxide, is parasitic \cite[Secs.~2.3 and 3.1]{taur2009}.

The oxide can be modeled as the dielectric in a capacitor. Its oxide capacitance per unit area is
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{ox}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}}{t_{\mathrm{ox}}},
\end{equation}
where \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide capacitance per unit area, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide permittivity, and \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) is the oxide thickness. The potential difference across the oxide is denoted by \(V_{\mathrm{ox}}\). Reducing \(t_{\mathrm{ox}}\) increases \(C_{\mathrm{ox}}\), thereby strengthening electrostatic control. However, the barrier width also decreases, increasing both the transmission probability and the gate-leakage current density under otherwise comparable material and bias conditions.
